Mathematical Definition

Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) involves minimizing an objective function $y$ defined by:

$$ y = \mathbf{x}^t \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x} $$

where $\mathbf{x} = (x_1, x_2, ..., x_n) \in \{0, 1\}^n$ is a vector of binary decision variables and $\mathbf{Q}$ is a symmetric matrix of constant coefficients.

Problem Formulation and Reduction

The objective is to solve constrained combinatorial problems in the form: $$ \begin{align} \min y &= \mathbf{x}^t \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x} \\ \text{subject to: } \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} &= \mathbf{b} \end{align} $$

where $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ consist of integer components representing system constraints. This formulation is flexible due to two key properties:

  • Linear to Quadratic: Any linear function in $x$ ($x_i$ binary) is quadratic without loss of generality because $x_i = x_i^2$ for $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$.
  • Order Reduction: Higher-dimensional functions can be reduced to quadratic forms by introducing penalty terms:
The Penalty Method

Constraints are incorporated into the QUBO objective function by adding quadratic penalties $P$. An inequality like $x_1 + x_2 \leq 1$ is transformed into an unconstrained minimization: $$ \min y = \mathbf{x}^t \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x} + P(x_1 x_2)^2 $$

  • Choosing $P > 0$ sufficiently large ensures that violating the constraint (setting $x_1=1, x_2=1$) is energetically unfavorable.
  • All penalty terms are squared to ensure the term is never negative.
Penalty Encoding Pairs

Common logical constraints and their corresponding quadratic penalties:

An example of Order Reduction

Let the objective function $y$ contain a cubic term $x_1 x_2 x_3$: $$ y = x_1 x_2 x_3 + x_2 x_3+ x_3 x_1 + ... $$ Define $y_1 = x_1 x_2$ so that $$ y = y_1 x_3 + x_2 x_3 + x_3 x_1 + ... $$ reducing $y$ to a quadratic function. Now it's necessary to add a penalty term, causing the solver to prefer the assignment $y_1 = x_1 x_2$ over any other assignment: $$ y = y_1 x_3 + x_2 x_3 + x_3 x_1 + ... + P(x_1 x_2 - 2x_1 y_1 - 2x_2 y_1 + 3y_1)^2 + \text{{other penalties}} $$ We can do this recursively for even higher order terms: $$ x_1 x_2 x_3 x_4 \rightarrow y_1 x_3 x_4 \rightarrow y_2 x_4 $$ with the corresponding penalty terms to enforce $y_1 = x_1 x_2$ and $y_2 = y_1 x_3$.

General Linear Inequalities and Slack Variables

To encode a general inequality $\sum_{i=1}^n a_i x_i \leq b$, introduce a slack variable $s$ such that $0 \leq s \leq b$. This converts the inequality into an equality: $$ \sum_{i=1}^n a_i x_i + s = b $$

To maintain binary consistency, $s$ is expanded using a power-of-two series: $$ s = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} 2^i s_i, \quad \text{where } N = \lfloor \log_2(b) \rfloor, \text{ and } s_i \in \{0, 1\} $$ Then the constraint is encoded as a penalty term of the following form: $$ P\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i x_i + \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} 2^i s_i - b\right)^2 $$ where the solver learns the value of $s$ that makes the term zero.

The General Form of QUBO

Let $\mathbf{x}$ be the vector of binary variables. Let $P_i g_i(\mathbf{x})$ be penalty terms accumulated from all constraints and order reductions, each scaled by a penalty coefficient $P_i > 0$. The general form of the QUBO is: $$ \min y = \mathbf{x}^t \mathbf{Q} \mathbf{x} + \sum_i P_i g_i(\mathbf{x}) $$

Conversion to Ising Model

The Ising model is a mathematical model of ferromagnetism in statistical mechanics. It uses spin variables $s_i \in \{-1, 1\}$. To convert a QUBO problem to an Ising model, apply the linear transformation:

$$ x_i = \frac{s_i + 1}{2} $$

where $x_i = 1$ corresponds to $s_i = 1$ (spin up) and $x_i = 0$ corresponds to $s_i = -1$ (spin down).

Transformation of the Objective Function

Substitute $x_i = \frac{s_i + 1}{2}$ into the quadratic form $x^t Q x$: $$ \begin{align} x_i x_j &= \frac{s_i + 1}{2} \cdot \frac{s_j + 1}{2} \\ &= \frac{1}{4} (s_i s_j + s_i + s_j + 1) \end{align} $$

The resulting expression takes the form of the Ising Hamiltonian: $$ H(s) = \sum_{i \lt j} J_{ij} s_i s_j + \sum_{i} h_i s_i + C $$ where $$ \begin{align} J_{ij} &= \frac{Q_{ij}}{4} \\ h_i &= \frac{Q_{ii}}{2} + \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{Q_{ij}}{4} \\ C &= \sum_i \frac{Q_{ii}}{2} + \sum_{i\lt j} \frac{Q_{ij}}{4} \end{align} $$

Parameters of the Ising Hamiltonian

The coefficients of the Ising model are derived from the QUBO matrix $Q$:

  • Couplings ($J_{ij}$): $J_{ij} = \frac{Q_{ij}}{4}$
  • External Fields ($h_i$): $h_i = \frac{Q_{ii}}{2} + \sum_{j \neq i} \frac{Q_{ij}}{4}$
  • Constant Offset ($C$): $C = \sum_i \frac{Q_{ii}}{2} + \sum_{i\lt j} \frac{Q_{ij}}{4}$

The constant $C$ shifts the energy spectrum but does not change the location of the minimum, meaning the ground state of the Ising model corresponds to the optimal solution of the QUBO.

Circuits and Hamiltonians

Quantum gates are physically implemented by performing time evolution under a carefully engineered Hamiltonian $H$. This transformation is described by the unitary operator: $$ U(H, t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar} $$

This connection allows us to describe quantum circuits in terms of Hamiltonians. However, implementing a circuit that exactly exponentiates a Hamiltonian with many non-commuting terms is challenging: $$ H = H_1 + H_2 + H_3 + \cdots + H_N $$

In such cases, we use the Trotter-Suzuki decomposition formula to approximate the evolution.

Trotter-Suzuki Decomposition

To implement an approximate time-evolution unitary for a Hamiltonian $H = \sum_k H_k$, we use the Trotter-Suzuki decomposition formula: $$ e^{A+B} \approx \left(e^{A/n} e^{B/n}\right)^n $$

This allows us to approximate the full evolution operator $U(H, t)$ by a product of simpler unitaries: $$ U(H, t, n) = \prod_{j=1}^n \prod_k e^{-iH_k t / n} $$

As the number of steps $n$ becomes larger, the operator $U(H, t, n)$ approaches the exact time-evolution operator $e^{-iHt}$.

QAOA

The Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) is a general technique to find approximate solutions to combinatorial optimization problems. It consists of the following steps:

  1. Define a cost Hamiltonian $H_C$ such that its ground state encodes the solution.
  2. Define a mixer Hamiltonian $H_M$.
  3. Construct the circuits $e^{-i\gamma H_C}$ and $e^{-i\alpha H_M}$ (cost and mixer layers).
  4. Build the circuit by repeating the layers: $$U(\gamma, \alpha) = e^{-i\alpha_n H_M} e^{-i\gamma_n H_C} \cdots e^{-i\alpha_1 H_M} e^{-i\gamma_1 H_C}$$
  5. Prepare an initial state, apply $U(\gamma, \alpha)$, and optimize parameters classically.
  6. Measure the output state to reveal approximate solutions to the problem.

Initialization and Adiabatic QAOA

We can think of QAOA as a Trotterization of the Quantum Adiabatic Algorithm.

  • Process: Start in the ground state $|+\rangle^{\otimes n}$ of the mixer Hamiltonian $X$ and move slowly to the ground state of the cost Hamiltonian $H_c$.
  • Adiabatic Initialization: We can initialize parameters based on this principle:
    • Move $\beta_i$ linearly from $1$ to $0$.
    • Move $\gamma_i$ linearly from $0$ to $1$.

By adopting this adiabatic initialization, the circuit can often find high-quality solutions even by skipping the classical optimization step.
